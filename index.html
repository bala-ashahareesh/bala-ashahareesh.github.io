Kernel Optimisation for NPU

Mapping Math to Intuition

In High school, my rebellious teenage phase forced me to believe that generalised conventions and equations were archaic forms of understanding math as they were devoid of any intuition. To me, if anything had to make sense, it needed to be intuitive. But I have grown to realise that despite being a great launcher for building motivation and appreciation for something, it should not be a self made ceiling wherein it prevents you from seeing how this form generalises over multiple domains.

And I guess fundamentally, that is what I will try to achieve through this blog. We will use intuition to formulate a theory and then feed it back to the equation itself, and then see how our intuition was a special case or implementation of a more general natural phenomenon.

Feature Space and Semantic Vectors

One of the most fundamental prerequisites we need before proceeding further is an intuition for an n-dimensional feature space. In such a space, every point can be interpreted as carrying semantic meaning, and every vector can be decomposed into a sum of components along different basis directions.

Ideally, we like to think of these basis directions as being independent or close to orthogonal, so that each coordinate reflects a different component of meaning. In the simple visual examples below, we attach easy human-readable meanings to those components. In real large language models, however, the learned basis functions are usually not so cleanly interpretable to us.

These labels are intentionally illustrative. We call the axes “Space”, “Romance”, and “Mystery” to make coordinates intuitive. Learned features in real language models need not coincide with individual human-readable concepts; semantic information can be distributed across many directions. An orthogonal coordinate basis is a mathematical convenience, not a claim that an LLM discovers three neat semantic axes.

Why the next diagrams use 2D: they keep the same vector-space mathematics, but restrict attention to two coordinates so the transformations, dot products, and crosstalk can be seen without a camera.

\[ x = c_1 b_1 + c_2 b_2 + c_3 b_3, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}^3. \]

So when we later speak about a key, query, or value being represented by a vector, what we really mean is that it is a point in such a feature space. Its coordinates tell us how much of each basis component is present.

Rediscovering SoftMax as a Kernel Function

When building intuition for understanding the attention mechanism, I like to think of standard self-attention as a divergence from a more natural mathematical progression: starting with a classical outer-product memory accumulation mechanism and gradually arriving at linear attention.

I bring this up here because the softmax function represents an interesting junction in this journey. It is at this point that we can either follow the path towards standard softmax self-attention or continue exploring linear attention, eventually leading us towards more advanced architectures such as Gated Delta Networks and Kimi Linear Attention, and further explorations into recurrent state-space models like Mamba.

Rather than starting with attention as a fully formed mechanism, I want to understand how these ideas emerge from simpler mathematical principles, what limitations motivate each transformation, and why different architectures take different paths.

So, we begin with the most fundamental building block: the memory update mechanism.

Ideally, when we think of "memory" as accumulated context or piece of information that is encoded in our d dimensional space as a vector, then it makes sense to think of it as a library that holds this info. To have no interference whatsoever, we would have the keys acting as an index that points to the location of the info (which we would call value). Since the keys are encoded as one-hot encoding, the position of the 1 is the position of the required value in the memory. Then the query would just be just be mathing that position.

\[ \text{Match}(q_i, k_j) = q_i^T k_j = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } i = j \\ 0 & \text{if } i \neq j \end{cases} \]
Example: One-Hot Keys (The Library)

Suppose our memory has 3 slots. The keys are just the standard basis vectors (one-hot encodings):

\(k_1 = [1, 0, 0]^T\), \(k_2 = [0, 1, 0]^T\), \(k_3 = [0, 0, 1]^T\)

If we want to retrieve the value at the second slot, our query is simply \(q = [0, 1, 0]^T\). Taking the dot product with the keys gives us:

\(q^T k_1 = 0\), \(q^T k_2 = 1\), \(q^T k_3 = 0\).

This perfectly selects the second value with a weight of 1, and zeroes out the rest.

Animation: one-hot keys — fill the slots, then query

As easy and intuitive as it sounds, the "library" analogy comes with the same limitation mathematically. If every book needs a new slot, every piece of info or value would also need a new slot. Imagine every piece of context updating the column count by 1. Therein lies the need for superposition. This allows us to take advantage of the d dimensional space more.

Superposition

As hinted above, now is a good time to put the library analogy to rest. To think of your books as being distributed across different slots such that each slot corresponds to different parts of different books is probably where I would draw the line and say it is a futile exercise to carry the analogy further. Although while typing this out, we could in fact push this further if we rephrase the analogy from referring to single books and towards topics. Now a "slot" or value can be a genre or theme, so that a book can be distributed among them. When we have a key that tells us which book we want, it can be seen as a weighted sum of different values or genres.

In this analogy: the Value (\(v\)) is the genre profile (e.g., how much Sci-Fi vs. Romance). The Key (\(k\)) is the thematic fingerprint or index (e.g., a mix of "Space" and "Castle"). The Memory (\(M\)) is the accumulated library.

Mathematically, if we have a value vector \(v \in \mathbb{R}^m\) and a distributed key vector \(k \in \mathbb{R}^n\), the memory update is an outer product \(v k^T\) (an \(m \times n\) matrix) accumulated into the memory state \(M\):

\[ M = \sum_{i=1}^{t} v_i k_i^T \]
Example A: Ideal Superposition (Orthogonal Keys)

Let's look at the ideal scenario where superposition works perfectly. Our values represent genres [Sci-Fi, Romance].

Book 1 is pure Sci-Fi: \(v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\). Its thematic key is a mix of Space and Aliens: \(k_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}\).
Book 2 is pure Romance: \(v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Its key is a mix of Castle and Drama: \(k_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -0.1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

Notice that the keys are distributed (not one-hot), but they are perfectly orthogonal (\(k_1^T k_2 = -0.1 + 0.1 = 0\)). The memory state accumulates as:

\[ M = v_1 k_1^T + v_2 k_2^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ -0.1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ -0.1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

If we query with \(q = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}\) (looking for Book 1), we get:

\[ M q = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.1 \\ -0.1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.01 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 1.01 v_1 + 0 v_2 \]

We retrieve perfectly clean Sci-Fi, with absolutely zero noise from Book 2. Superposition is powerful if we can keep the keys orthogonal.

Example A.1: Normalising Two Keys and a Query

Since a key acts as an index, moving from one-hot encoding to superposition makes more sense if we imagine the key vector as distributing its influence across the dimensions. In the outer-product update \(vk^T\), the signed key coordinates prescribe how much of the value is written into each column.

Let us return to both keys from the orthogonal superposition example: \(k_1=[1,0.1]^T\) and \(k_2=[-0.1,1]^T\). Their orientations are different, yet both are just a little longer than one unit. RMS normalisation gives the two indexing directions a common scale.

\[ \operatorname{RMS}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{x_1^2+x_2^2}{2}}, \qquad \widetilde{x}=\frac{x}{\operatorname{RMS}(x)}. \]

For either key, \(\operatorname{RMS}(k_i)=\sqrt{0.505}\approx0.7106\). Thus:

\[ \widetilde{k}_1\approx\begin{bmatrix}1.407\\0.141\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \widetilde{k}_2\approx\begin{bmatrix}-0.141\\1.407\end{bmatrix}. \]

Their directions and orthogonality do not change. Both endpoints move radially onto the circle \(\|x\|_2=\sqrt{2}\), because RMSNorm without learned gain or epsilon sets \(\sum_i\widetilde{x}_i^2=2\) in two dimensions.

Animation: Two indexing directions, one RMSNorm circle

What exactly is being distributed? A memory update uses the signed coordinates of the key, including the negative first component of \(k_2\). When we speak of its geometric “energy,” we mean the squared components \(k_{ij}^2/\|k_i\|_2^2\). In this example, \(k_1\) has about 99% of its squared-coordinate energy along the first axis, while \(k_2\) has about 99% along the second. RMSNorm standardises their lengths; it does not turn their coordinates into probabilities.

Continuing with normalised keys. In the next animation, we rebuild the same memory using \(\widetilde{k}_1\) and \(\widetilde{k}_2\), and query it using \(\widetilde{q}=\widetilde{k}_1\). The directions and orthogonality are unchanged, but because RMSNorm gives each 2D vector squared length \(2\), the target retrieval coefficient becomes \(2\) rather than \(1.01\). This is a change of scale, not a change of which book wins.

\[ \widetilde M=\sum_{i=1}^{2}v_i\widetilde{k}_i^T =\sqrt{\frac{2}{1.01}}\begin{bmatrix}1&0.1\\-0.1&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \widetilde M\widetilde q = 2v_1+0v_2. \]
Animation: superposition using RMS-normalised keys and query

This gives us a beautiful associativity in the memory update.

If we retrieve from our memory state \(M\) using query \(q\), we get \(M q = \sum v_i (k_i^T q) = \sum v_i \cdot \text{score}_i\). Ideally, we want our scoring mechanism to behave as a selector: the desired value should have a weight of \(1\), and all undesired values should have a weight of \(0\) (a one-hot vector).

Normalising Retrieval Scores into Relative Weights

There are now two distinct forms of normalisation in our story. RMSNorm rescales a key or query vector so that its length is controlled. What we need next is different: once a query produces scores against many memories, how do we turn those scores into a relative share of the output?

From the outer-product memory, we have \(Mq=\sum_i s_i v_i\), where \(s_i=q^T k_i\). This is already a weighted sum, but the scores do not automatically represent percentages. Suppose for a moment that every score is nonnegative and their total \(Z=\sum_i s_i\) is positive. Then we can divide each score by the total:

\[s_i=q^Tk_i,\qquad Z=\sum_j s_j,\qquad w_i=\frac{s_i}{Z},\qquad \sum_i w_i=1.\] \[\bar y=\sum_i w_i v_i=\frac{Mq}{Z}.\]

Now each \(w_i\) is a fraction of the total score: multiply by 100 to read it as a percentage. This does not change which memory has the larger score, and it does not remove unwanted contributions. It changes the overall scale so that we can interpret the result as an average of the values.

The limitation: the percentage interpretation works in this simple form only for nonnegative scores with a positive total. Dot products can be negative, and dividing by a sum can then produce negative weights or even be undefined. More importantly, it does nothing to separate nearly equal scores. We will encounter that failure next, then look for a kernel that can address it.

When Keys Overlap: The Limits of Score Normalisation

Normalising the total score makes the sum easier to interpret, but it cannot tell whether that score came from the memory we wanted or from an overlapping neighbour. For the dot-product kernel \(\kappa(q,k)=q^Tk\), the actual retrieval remains a weighted combination of all stored values. What happens when our key directions are not orthogonal?

Even if the correct memory has the highest score, the other scores may remain large. Their value contributions interfere with the desired result. This is visible in the following counterexample and its affine-noise interpretation.

Example B: The Reality (Crosstalk & Noise)

For this next counterexample, we return temporarily to raw coordinates so that the overlap parameter α can be varied directly. The crosstalk phenomenon remains with normalised directions; normalising alone cannot remove overlap.

In reality, thematic fingerprints overlap heavily. We cannot always keep the keys orthogonal. Let's use overlapping keys:

What is α? We use α as a knob for cross-coordinate overlap: \(k_1=[1,\alpha]^T\), \(k_2=[\alpha,1]^T\). At \(\alpha=0\) the keys are orthogonal. As α grows, each key borrows more of the other coordinate and the two basis directions lean together. At \(\alpha=1\) they are identical. α is not the cosine similarity: here \(\cos\theta=\frac{2\alpha}{1+\alpha^2}\). Example B uses \(\alpha=0.5\), so \(\cos\theta=0.8\).

Book 1 (Mostly Sci-Fi): \(v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 \\ 0.2 \end{bmatrix}\). Key (Space + Aliens): \(k_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\).
Book 2 (Mostly Romance): \(v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0.9 \end{bmatrix}\). Key (Space + Romance): \(k_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\).

The memory state \(M\) becomes a messy superposition of both.

Any query then matches with the key via inner product to give a score that decides how desired a particular value is. Let's query with \(q = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\) (looking for Book 1):

Score for Book 1 (\(k_1\)): \(q^T k_1 = (1 \times 1) + (0.5 \times 0.5) = 1.25\)

Score for Book 2 (\(k_2\)): \(q^T k_2 = (1 \times 0.5) + (0.5 \times 1) = 1.0\)

Book 1 does win, 1.25 vs 1.0. Dividing by their sum produces relative weights of 55.6% and 44.4%. But Book 2 still gets almost as much influence. Indeed, \(Mq=1.25v_1+1.0v_2=[1.10,1.15]^T\) favours Romance. Scaling by \(1/2.25\) produces \([0.489,0.511]^T\): the output is now an average, but it still favours Romance. The noise has not disappeared.

Animation: crosstalk — the highest score still loses the vote

That is the core limitation of the plain dot product as a kernel, and it comes in three flavours:

  • It does not sharpen. Similar keys get similar scores (1.25 vs 1.0), so the winner does not take all. We wanted a selector, but we got a gentle weighting.
  • Losers still contribute, and they add up. Every undesired item leaves a small non-zero weight. Individually harmless, but summed over many items (or one heavily overlapping item) they can outweigh the desired value.
  • Simple score normalisation does not sharpen. Dividing each positive score by their sum changes the total scale, but not their relative ratios. A weaker match can still contribute almost as much as the strongest match.

Even after turning these scores into fractions, the relative interference remains. We therefore need something more than a shared denominator: a scoring transformation that distinguishes close matches before assigning their final weights.

The Affine Noise Problem

If the desired signal is \(x\), and our desired weight is \(m\), the ideal output is \(y = mx\). However, the sum of undesired scores introduces a constant bias \(c\). Thus, the actual output is an affine equation: \(y = mx + c\).

The Need to Differentiate Nearby Scores

Draft explanation — refine this in your own words

We have made retrieval scores interpretable as relative shares when they are nonnegative. But Example B reveals the next limitation: the correct memory can receive only a slightly larger share than an irrelevant neighbour. Dividing all scores by the same total cannot change their ratios or resolve that ambiguity.

For example, imagine three books receiving similarity scores 1.00, 0.97, and 0.94. The first is the best match, yet dividing by their sum gives approximately 34.4%, 33.3%, and 32.3%. The ranking is correct, but the retrieval is still blurry: almost everyone gets the same vote.

What we need is not another change of vector length, nor simply another common denominator. We need a new scoring kernel or transformation that can increase the relative contrast between competing memories, then produce nonnegative weights that sum to one.

That motivates a new scoring rule: exponentiate the scores, and divide by their total. The exponential makes a score gap count multiplicatively; the denominator turns the results into a distribution. We arrive at softmax not because it is a magical lookup function, but because we want a tunable, smooth competition among memories.

Bridge to the next section: a smooth competition is not yet a perfect one-hot lookup. We can make it sharper, but the choice of sharpness matters: a very sharp distribution can be decisive and brittle, while a soft one can preserve useful ambiguity.

\[ s_i=\kappa(q,k_i),\qquad w_i(\tau)=\frac{e^{s_i/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{s_j/\tau}},\quad \tau>0. \] Here \(\tau\) is the temperature. Smaller \(\tau\) produces sharper competition; larger \(\tau\) keeps the weights more even.
